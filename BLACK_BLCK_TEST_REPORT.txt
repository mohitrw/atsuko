PART 1: PHONETIC & ACOUSTIC ANALYSIS OF "BLACK" / "BLCK."
================================================================================

Phonetic Representation:
  - Spoken Pronunciation: /ˈblæk/
  - Syllable Count: 1 Syllable (Monosyllabic)
  - Stress: Single primary stressed syllable

PHONEME-BY-PHONEME BREAKDOWN:

1. Onset Cluster: /bl/ (Voiced Bilabial Plosive /b/ + Voiced Alveolar Lateral /l/)
   - Frequency Concentration: 150 Hz - 600 Hz (Low-Frequency Register).
   - Acoustic Weakness:
     The /b/ stop is voiced and low-frequency. In a music application, low
     frequencies are completely saturated by:
       • Subwoofer kick drums and basslines (40 Hz - 250 Hz)
       • Room rumble and computer/ceiling fans (80 Hz - 200 Hz)
     In a crowded room or while your music is playing at medium-to-high volume,
     the microphone frequently misses the initial /bl/ onset because it is
     masked beneath the bass floor.

2. Nucleus Vowel: /æ/ (Short Open-Front Unrounded Vowel)
   - Formant Energy: F1 ~750 Hz, F2 ~1750 Hz.
   - Acoustic Strength: Strong resonant vocal tract energy. Provides decent
     clarity in quiet environments.

3. Coda: /k/ (Voiceless Velar Plosive Stop)
   - Acoustic Signature: 45ms - 70ms of silent occlusion followed by a transient
     burst at 1,800 Hz - 3,200 Hz.
   - Acoustic Weakness compared to "NX" (/ks/):
     A simple /k/ stop lacks the sustained high-frequency fricative hiss
     (4,000 Hz - 8,000 Hz) found in "NX" (/ks/), "Frost" (/st/), or "Dash" (/ʃ/).
     It does not cut through room noise as effectively as the razor-sharp 'X'.

PART 2: SPEECH RECOGNITION (STT) BEHAVIOR: "BLACK" vs "BLCK."

     frequent adjective in English).
   - Google Web Speech API (en-US) recognizes spoken /ˈblæk/ as "black" virtually
     100% of the time in quiet rooms.
   - Unlike "Atsuko", Google will not mangle "black" into weird random words.

2. WHAT HAPPENS WITH THE SPELLING "BLCK.":
   - In written text, "blck." looks extremely stylish, modern, and minimalist.
   - HOWEVER, Speech-to-Text engines (like Google Web Speech) DO NOT output
     abbreviations or punctuation from spoken voice.
   - When a human speaks the word "blck.", the vocal cords say "black".
   - The STT engine transcribes: "black" (never "blck" or "blck.").
   - To make "blck." work in code, the app must listen for the word "black".
   - Therefore, "blck." behaves identically to "black" in live voice recognition.
