2. THE SIMILARITY CHECK LOOP (Lines 298 - 306):

     if (calculateSimilarity(w, target) >= 0.60) {
       return { hasWakeWord: true, matchedToken: w, command: after };
     }
   }

3. THE MATHEMATICAL MATCH:
   When you say "sure", Google Speech transcribes the word as: "sure".
   The code compares "sure" (4 letters) against every word in targetKeywords.
   When it compares "sure" with "asuke":

     Target:   a - s - u - k - e  (5 letters)
     Spoken:       s - u - r - e  (4 letters)

     - Letter 'a' is deleted       -> Cost = 1
     - Letter 's' matches 's'      -> Cost = 0
     - Letter 'u' matches 'u'      -> Cost = 0
     - Letter 'k' replaced by 'r'  -> Cost = 1
     - Letter 'e' matches 'e'      -> Cost = 0

     Total Difference (Edit Distance) = 2.
     Maximum Length = 5.

     Formula: 1 - (Edit Distance / Max Length)
     Similarity = 1 - (2 / 5) = 1 - 0.40 = 0.60 (EXACTLY 60%).

4. THE TRIGGER:
   The threshold in the code is:
   if (calculateSimilarity(w, target) >= 0.60)

   Because 0.60 is equal to 0.60, the code says:
   "MATCH! The user said a wake word!"

   So whenever you say:
   - "sure"
   - "make sure" (the loop checks "make", then checks "sure" -> matches!)
   - "are you sure"
   - "for sure"
   - "sure thing"

   The voice assistant thinks you said "asuke" and instantly pops up!

PART 2: WHY "SURE" WORKS 10/10 TIMES, BUT "ASUKO" ONLY WORKS 3-4 TIMES OUT OF 10
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Why does a random word like "sure" work every single time, while the actual name
"Asuko" only works 3 to 4 times out of 10?

There are two major reasons:

REASON 1: GOOGLE'S SPEECH RECOGNIZER IS SET TO ENGLISH (en-US)
- "Sure" is one of the most common, everyday English words in existence.
  Google's Speech Recognition AI has heard millions of English speakers say "sure".
  Its neural network gives the word "sure" a 99.9% probability score.
  So whenever you say "sure", Google writes "sure" instantly and flawlessly.

- On the other hand, "Asuko" is NOT an English dictionary word.
  When you say "Asuko", Google's English AI gets confused. It tries to force what
  it heard into real English words that sound similar:
  - Sometimes Google writes: "a suit"
  - Sometimes Google writes: "a sock"
  - Sometimes Google writes: "a suck"
  - Sometimes Google writes: "issue"
  - Sometimes Google writes: "a soon"
  - Sometimes Google writes: "assumed"
  - Sometimes Google writes: "a sub"

  We tested all of these against our code:
  - "a suit"   -> Fails the similarity test! (Missed)
  - "a sock"   -> Fails the similarity test! (Missed)
  - "a suck"   -> Fails the similarity test! (Missed)
  - "issue"    -> Fails the similarity test! (Missed)
  - "a soon"   -> Fails the similarity test! (Missed)
  - "assumed"  -> Fails the similarity test! (Missed)
  - "a sub"    -> Fails the similarity test! (Missed)

  Only 3 to 4 times out of 10 does Google write: "asuko", "a suko", "ask who",
  or "as who" (which our code catches). The other 6 to 7 times, Google writes
  one of the above English words, and the assistant stays silent!

REASON 2: ACOUSTIC SOUND ENERGY
- The word "sure" starts with a loud, sharp "SH" sound (high-frequency friction).
  Even with fan noise, music, or from across the room, the microphone picks up
  "SH" very clearly.
- The word "Asuko" starts with a quiet, soft vowel "Ah" or "Uh".
  In a normal room, the microphone often misses the quiet "Ah" and only catches
  the second half, leading Google to hear something distorted.
